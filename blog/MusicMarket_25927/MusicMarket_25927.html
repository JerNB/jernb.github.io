Two years ago, when I was sixteen, I stepped into the office of AllSaints Music Tech at the invitation of a classmate. It didn’t look like a traditional record label, full of trophies and celebrity portraits, nor did it resemble the kind of scrappy startup I had imagined, crowded with whiteboards and endless KPIs. Back then, I had no idea I would eventually become so interested in the intersection of music and data. I just remember sensing a strange sense of order in the room.

Two years have passed. I’ve since organized concerts in and outside of school, researched and written business analysis reports, and tried to use data and modeling to understand why people fall in love with certain songs. Looking back, that visit felt like a seed that took a long time to sprout. Now I feel ready to say something about it.

How the music market has changed

The music streaming landscape today looks very different. Exclusive copyrights are fading out. Platforms no longer build barriers by hoarding catalogs. Big prepayments are drying up — nobody wants to pour massive resources into a track that might not take off. Copyright is no longer a strategic weapon; it’s become basic supply.

In this new structure, competition now revolves around three things: who controls the distribution endpoints at the system level, who defines and maintains technical standards, and who can turn data into experience — legally and meaningfully. These changes may sound technical on the surface, but they reflect a fundamental shift: from a focus on content itself, to how content is distributed, experienced, and measured. (Thinking about it now, this lines up quite a bit with the ideas I explored in my article about the service industry)

Rethinking that day at AllSaints Music

With this new perspective, I began rethinking what I saw that day at AllSaints. At the time, I just found the company “unusual.” Now I see the logic behind it. They weren’t trying to grab user attention or play the traffic game. Instead, they quietly embedded themselves deep into the systems of phone manufacturers — OPPO, vivo, Xiaomi — building up capabilities like music playback, recommendation, user experience, and royalty settlement from the ground up.

It might sound like they’re far removed from the stage, far from the music itself — but in reality, they help decide how sound travels into our ears and what sticks.

I started to see the system behind the sound

From a business model standpoint, AllSaints isn’t just stitching together random features — it’s built on a clear causal chain. Upstream, they collaborate with record labels and independent artists, providing platforms for authentication, distribution, and promotion. In the middle, they use AI to boost audio efficiency while ensuring transparent royalties and legal compliance. On the front end, they reach users through native apps in phone systems, headphone integrations, and in-car audio environments — earning revenue from both B2B partnerships and C-side subscriptions and ads. It’s the kind of infrastructure play you’d expect from a cloud service provider, not a music company.

One of their biggest bets is spatial audio. As a band vocalist, I’ve always been sensitive to sound — not just emotionally, but now also technically. During rehearsals, I often wonder: if a song were heard in spatial audio, would it be loved more widely? But I also know this — unless users can feel the difference, even the most polished sound enhancements are just marketing. AllSaints reminded me that even something as intuitive as “listening to music” sits atop layers of engineering. What we call “good sound” may actually depend on codec details, headphone optimization, or even a recommendation parameter buried in a system.

Music is no longer just music

Seeing what AllSaints was building made me realize something: music isn’t just about the relationship between songs and listeners. It’s a dense, multi-dimensional network — of creators, platforms, devices, algorithms, and regulations. AllSaints isn’t the only company working on this, but it gives us a window into something essential: in an industry obsessed with virality and noise, if a company can survive — and even grow — without flashy stories or celebrity endorsements, just by building robust systems and workflows… that deserves attention.

A conclusion, or maybe just the beginning

This piece is really a response to that visit two years ago.

Back then, I didn’t have the language to describe what “audio as infrastructure” meant. I couldn’t yet explain how embedding music into systems could reshape the entire listening experience. But now I see it. I see that music today isn’t just published and played. It’s negotiated, routed, processed, and calculated. The “technical details” we once ignored now have the power to shape a song’s fate. And the measure of a music product isn’t whether its UI is cool, but whether it lets people create, share, and feel — easily and honestly.

As someone who leads a band and has built my own small platform and website, I’ve come to realize that the real bottlenecks in music aren’t just about writing better songs or finding bigger audiences. Often, they’re about broken recommendation systems and disconnected ecosystems. TikTok’s hit songs are a perfect example — fast to rise, fast to fade. They’re optimized as sonic products, not genuine expressions.

And the future of music? It shouldn’t just be about AI composition or NFT collectibles. It should be about systems that let great work be seen, experiences that actually feel different, and infrastructures that make the whole process more human.

I don’t know if AllSaints will change the industry. I don’t even know where the market is really heading. But I do know that companies like this remind me of one thing: music doesn’t just live in melodies — it lives in structure. Writing this article wasn’t about analyzing a company. It was about capturing the shift in how I think. And maybe that shift, more than any analysis, is what really matters.
