In recent years, the efficiency dividends of China’s manufacturing sector have been gradually exhausted. This is a fact, not a forecast. Against this backdrop, we’re forced to ask a foundational question: where will the next phase of structural growth come from? My answer is services — specifically, human-centered services that are deeply embedded in daily life. These are the services we use every day, often without noticing, yet they shape the texture of how society functions.

This isn’t an intuitive guess. It’s a logical conclusion that emerges from structural trends, demographic shifts, and the evolving nature of demand. Over the past decade, we maximized the flow of goods through the internet and supply chain optimization. But that path is nearing its natural limit. What comes next is not more products, but a redefinition of what it means to be taken care of. It’s no longer about whether deliveries arrive on time — it’s about whether an elderly parent feels safe stepping outside, or whether a child can access high-quality guidance at the right time.

All of these questions point toward service density, experience quality, and the ability to coordinate between people and machines.

So — what is the service sector, really?

The question is simple, but I believe it’s worth revisiting. The clearest way to grasp the true size of services is to break down a single day. The ride you book when you wake up, the breakfast you order, the appointment you schedule online, the asset management dashboard you check, the care provided to your aging parents, the frictionless retail experience, the alert from your bank about account security — none of these are peripheral. They’re the infrastructure of urban life. We’re moving from owning things to experiencing services. That shift is the core logic behind the rise of a service-driven economy.

More importantly, there are three long-term structural forces already in motion.

First, traditional retail efficiencies — boosted by the internet — have plateaued. Traffic, conversion, ARPU — every optimization lever has been pulled. The next growth driver must come from upgrading the service experience itself.

Second, population aging is accelerating. As of 2024, China has over 310 million people aged 60 and above. By 2035, that number is expected to surpass 450 million. What’s coming is not a one-time demand surge but a long-term, multi-layered need for sustained services. This is the definition of a “long slope, deep snow” market.

Third, society is shifting its priority from growth to stability. Stability requires jobs, and the service sector is by far the strongest job-absorbing engine. These three variables aren’t momentary — they’re path-dependent and unlikely to reverse.

International comparisons make the room for growth even clearer. As of 2024, the service sector accounts for 56.7% of China’s GDP. In the EU, it’s closer to 75%. In the United States, the number reaches 81.8%. These aren’t just gaps — they reflect different stages of economic maturity. In other words, China has at least ten to twenty years of room to grow into its own service economy. And this path doesn’t require betting. It just requires disciplined execution.

Let’s look at one particular frontier: the aging economy

In 2024, 29.3% of Japan’s population is 65 or older. This has forced the emergence of a newly designed service system — from neighborhood-based care to in-home rehabilitation, from age-friendly tourism to financial protection. While China’s demographic structure isn’t yet identical, we’re rapidly approaching similar territory — with a much larger window of opportunity. This means that if we approach services with the same rigor as product design, we could simultaneously meet demand and unlock new jobs and entrepreneurial opportunities. It’s not driven by sentiment. The data is simply becoming too loud to ignore.

In this context, the role of technology is shifting behind the scenes. It’s no longer about extracting another wave of attention or user acquisition. Instead, it’s becoming the operating engine of the service stack. Future service flows will be shaped by a mix of algorithmic dispatch, sensor recognition, robotic collaboration, and human empathy. Hospitals, neighborhoods, eldercare centers, the family home — each of these deserves to be broken down and rebuilt. What matters is not cold automation, but systems that free people from repetitive tasks so they can focus on the emotional and interpretive work that machines cannot do. At its core, service is still about human connection. The only difference is that its efficiency and consistency must now be leveled up.

So how do we tell if a service scenario has real value?

I think there are three key questions.

First, has the user experience been meaningfully compressed in time? For example, from waiting three days to getting a response in three hours — from uncertainty to instant expectations.

Second, is the payment process seamless — especially for elderly users navigating a patchwork of public insurance, private insurance, and out-of-pocket systems? If they can’t resolve it with one click, the friction is real.

Third, are service professionals being empowered, not replaced? Can they make better decisions with the help of systems, or are they stuck in repetitive, manual tasks? If a service system passes all three tests, it likely has compound value.

That said, we shouldn’t romanticize the space. Services are labor-intensive, low-margin, difficult to scale across regions, and often subject to fragmented regulation. But these aren’t dead ends. They’re design challenges. The path forward starts with standardizing processes, enforcing quality control, and building layered credentialing systems. Ironically, it’s often in the overlooked corners — where people assume things are too hard or too small — that standards and trust, once built, scale into brands. And from there, repeat usage and word-of-mouth create the most powerful form of organic growth.

If we want to further validate this sector’s potential, we should track three signals: whether the service sector’s share of GDP is rising steadily; whether per capita spending — especially in healthcare, education, and travel — is gaining share within household budgets; and whether new service jobs are emerging that align with the real needs of an aging society, like care coordinators, fraud-prevention counselors, or home recovery coaches. If those signals trend upward, long-term returns will follow.

In the end, my view is simple.

If more people in a society need to be seen, supported, and clearly understood, then services stop being an industry. They become infrastructure. And the value of infrastructure isn’t how quickly it pays back — it’s that, once built, it allows everything else to run. You don’t stabilize a society by building more factories. You stabilize it by making sure the services around people’s daily lives are dignified, predictable, and real. For the last 40 years, China’s focus has been growth. The next chapter is about stability. And stability comes from the ordinary moments of being seen and being served. Let systems handle the complexity. Let people deliver the warmth. That’s the real direction for China’s service economy.
